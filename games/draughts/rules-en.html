Draughts (Checkers) Rules: Mandatory Capture, Multi-Jumps, and Kinging

2026-11-09 · 7 min read

The game has two names in English: draughts in Britain, checkers in the United States. Both refer to the same 8×8 board with 12 pieces a side. The rules are short, but one of them changes how every move is chosen: if a capture exists, you must take it.

The board and the starting position

Play on an 8×8 board. Pieces sit only on the 32 dark squares. Each side starts with 12 pieces, placed on the three rows nearest that player. White starts at the bottom and black at the top, and white moves first.

This page describes English draughts（英式跳棋）, also called American checkers. It is not the 10×10 international game, and it is not Chinese checkers, which is a jumping race on a star-shaped board with no captures.

How a man moves

A man（兵）moves one square diagonally forward. White men move up the board, black men move down. Each turn, a man may move to either forward diagonal if that square is empty. A man cannot move sideways or backward.

When a man reaches the far row, it is crowned a king（王）. Promotion happens immediately, even in the middle of a capture sequence. White promotes on the eighth row; black promotes on the first.

Capturing: jump over, land beyond

To capture, an opponent's piece must be on the adjacent diagonal square, and the square directly beyond it on the same diagonal must be empty. Jump over the piece and land beyond it. The jumped piece is removed from the board.

A man can capture only forward, in the same two diagonal directions it uses to move. It cannot jump backward.

Example. A white man on c3 faces a black man on d4, and e5 is empty. The white man jumps d4 and lands on e5. The black man on d4 is removed. If e5 were occupied, the jump would not be available.

Mandatory capture（必须吃子）

If any capture is available on your turn, you must make one. Ordinary diagonal moves are illegal while a capture exists. This applies to every piece on the board, not just the piece you wanted to move, and it applies to kings as well.

You are not required to choose the capture that takes the most pieces. English draughts requires that you capture, but it lets you choose among the available capture routes. A one-piece capture is legal even when a three-piece capture is also available. This is one of the rules that separates English draughts from international draughts, where the longest capture is compulsory.

The practical effect is simple. At the start of every turn, scan the whole board for jumps before you consider a quiet move. If you see one, a quiet move is not an option.

Multi-jumps（连吃）

After a capture, the same piece may be able to jump again from its landing square. If it can, it must continue. The turn does not end until the piece has no further capture available. This is a multi-jump, or chain capture.

The piece may change direction between jumps. It can turn a corner, provided each new jump is diagonal, each victim is an opponent's piece, and each landing square is empty. It may not jump the same piece twice, and it may not land on a square it has already used in the same sequence.

You may not stop halfway. Jumping one piece and then stopping because the position looks good is not allowed while another jump remains. On this site you choose the final landing square, and the full path is shown before the move is made.

There is one promotion detail worth knowing. If a man reaches the far row during a multi-jump, it is crowned immediately and the turn ends. It does not continue jumping as a king, even if a king capture would be available from that square.

How a king moves and captures

A king moves one square diagonally in any of the four diagonal directions. It may capture forward or backward. The jump is still a single jump over one adjacent piece to the empty square directly beyond it.

A king is not a flying king. It cannot slide several empty squares along a diagonal, and it cannot capture a piece from a distance. That longer-range movement belongs to 10×10 international draughts, not to this 8×8 game.

In the endgame, one king is usually worth about three men, because it can attack from both directions and is much harder to trap.

Winning and drawing

You win in two ways. Your opponent has no pieces left, or your opponent has pieces but no legal move on their turn. Having pieces and being blocked is still a loss, not a draw.

A draw can happen when neither side has pieces left, which is rare. The more common draw rule is the no-progress rule: if 40 moves pass with no capture and no man move, the game is drawn. In this implementation the counter is kept in half-moves, so the game ends after 80 half-moves without progress. A king move counts as no progress, because only a capture or a man move resets the counter.

The three mistakes beginners make

1. Playing a quiet move while a capture exists

This is the most common illegal move. A player sees an empty square ahead and pushes a man, missing a jump on the other side of the board. The fix is a habit: before every move, look for jumps first, then consider everything else.

2. Stopping a multi-jump early

A player jumps one piece and stops, satisfied with the trade. If another jump is available from the landing square, the move is incomplete. The game will force the continuation, so it is better to plan the whole chain before you start it.

3. Treating kings like men

Kings move and capture in four directions, while men move and capture in only two. Assuming a king can fly across the board is the opposite mistake: in this game it moves one square at a time. Knowing exactly what a king can reach is the difference between a safe back row and a lost endgame.

A simple way to practise

Before each move, do three checks in order. First, count your own captures. Second, count the captures your opponent would have after your intended move. Third, look at whether your move leaves a piece with an empty square directly behind it, which is the shape that allows a jump.

On defence, keep your pieces connected on diagonals and avoid leaving a piece with nothing behind it. On attack, try to create two capture threats at once, so the opponent can block only one. When you can see two moves ahead reliably, the higher AI levels become playable rather than random.

Common questions

Can I make an ordinary move when a capture is available?

No. If any capture exists on your turn, you must take one. Ordinary diagonal steps are illegal until no capture remains. This is the mandatory capture rule.

Can a multi-jump stop in the middle?

No. After each jump, if the same piece can jump again, it must continue. The turn ends only when no further jump is possible. You may choose which capture route to take, but once started you must finish it.

Does a king move like a flying king?

No. In this 8×8 game a king moves one square diagonally in any direction and may capture forward or backward. It does not slide several empty squares like the king in 10×10 international draughts.
