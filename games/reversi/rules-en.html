Reversi (Othello) Rules and Strategy: Why Corners Win Games

2026-11-09 · 8 min read

Reversi is the rare game where the player with more discs is often losing. The rules fit on a card. The strategy does not, because every flip changes who controls the board. This guide covers the complete rules of Reversi（黑白棋，also known as Othello）, then explains why corners decide most games and what the best moves actually look like.

The board and the opening position

Reversi is played on an 8×8 board, 64 squares. The standard opening uses four discs in the center: white on d4 and e5, black on e4 and d5. Black moves first. Because the opening already occupies four squares, a game lasts at most 60 moves.

This guide uses a1 for the bottom-left square and h8 for the top-right, the same convention chess players use.

How a move works: flank and flip

On your turn, place one disc on an empty square. That square must form a line with one of your existing discs, in one of eight directions: horizontal, vertical, or either diagonal.

In that line, every square between the new disc and your old disc must hold opponent discs, with no empty square and no extra disc of yours in between. Any opponent discs caught in that line flip to your color.

One move can flip in several directions at once. A square that flips nothing is not a legal move（合法点）. In the opening, black has exactly four legal moves: c4, d3, e6 and f5.

Example. You play a black disc on c5. To the left, the row reads black-new, white-b4, black-a4. The white disc on b4 flips. If a second line also runs through c5 and ends in a black disc, every white disc on that line flips too.

The important detail is the empty square. A gap breaks the line. Two of your own discs in a row also break it, because only the discs between the new disc and the first disc of yours can flip.

Passing, ending and scoring

If you have no legal move, you pass（停一手）. Your opponent moves again. A pass is not a loss and does not cost you a turn permanently; it simply skips you while the board offers you nothing.

The game ends in one of two ways. All 64 squares are full, or neither player has a legal move. Count the discs. The larger total wins, and 32-32 is a draw.

This is the first surprise for new players. Positions are scored at the end, not during play. A player who leads 40-24 with ten empty squares can still lose, because a single endgame sequence may flip a large group.

Why corners win

A disc can be flipped only if the line continues past it and ends at another disc of the opponent's color. A corner has no square beyond it. No row, column or diagonal continues outside the board, so nothing can bracket a corner from behind. Once a corner is yours, it stays yours.

That makes a corner a stable disc（稳定子）: a disc that can never be flipped again. A full edge is stable in the same way. If you own a1 and every square from a1 to h1 is black, that entire row is permanent. It also anchors the discs just inside the edge, which is why taking a corner and then expanding along the two edges is more valuable than taking a corner and stopping.

Corners also create a base for the endgame. From a corner you can build inward, and those discs are hard to attack. Two corners usually mean the game is close to decided. All four corners almost always means a lopsided score.

The X-squares and C-squares

The four squares diagonally next to a corner are called X-squares（X 点）: b2, g2, b7 and g7. They are the classic beginner trap. If you place a disc on b2 and your opponent already has a disc on c3, they can play a1, bracket your b2 disc along the diagonal, and take the corner. Your move handed them the corner.

The squares directly beside a corner are C-squares（C 点）: a2, b1, g1, h2 and their mirrors. They are less dangerous than X-squares because they do not automatically give away the corner, but they often give the opponent a way to attack the edge. Treat both as squares to avoid until the corner is already yours or you can see that the opponent cannot reach it.

Mobility: the score that is not on the board

Disc count is the final score. Mobility（行动力）is the score during the game. Mobility means the number of legal moves you have and, more importantly, the number your opponent has.

A move that flips six discs and gives the opponent five new legal moves is usually worse than a move that flips one disc and leaves the opponent with two. Every legal move is a choice. Fewer choices means the opponent must eventually play a bad square, often an X-square or C-square, and that is how corners are handed over.

This is why strong players often keep their discs clustered in the center and avoid flipping along the edges early. A compact group limits the opponent's options. A disc pushed to the edge opens new lines and new squares for the other side.

Parity and the endgame count

When the board is nearly full, the game becomes a counting problem. Divide the empty squares into separate regions. Look at the number of empty squares in each region and whose turn it is to enter it.

If a region has an odd number of empty squares and you move first into it, you will usually take the last move in that region. If it has an even number, the opponent usually takes the last move. The player who takes the last move in more regions gets the final flips. This idea is called parity（奇偶）.

You do not need to calculate every region perfectly. Near the end, simply count the empty squares in the corners and along the edges. Choosing the region that gives you the last move is often worth more than flipping a few extra discs immediately.

What are the best Reversi moves?

There is no single best move without a board position, but the priorities are stable across most games:

  1. Take a safe corner. A corner you can keep is almost always worth taking. A corner you reach through an X-square is usually a gift to the opponent.
  2. Deny mobility. Prefer the move that leaves the opponent with the fewest legal replies, even if it flips fewer discs.
  3. Stay central early. Keep your discs together in the middle. Avoid flipping along the edges before you control a corner.
  4. Build from the corner. After taking a corner, extend along the adjacent edges so the stable group grows.
  5. Count the endgame. In the last ten moves, count empty squares by region and take the last move where it matters.

The best Reversi move is usually the one that improves your position after the opponent replies, not the one that looks biggest right now.

Common questions

Does black always move first in Reversi?

Yes. The four opening discs are already placed, with white on d4 and e5 and black on e4 and d5. Black moves first, and players alternate from there.

Can a disc on a corner ever be flipped?

No. To flip a disc, the line must pass through it and end at another disc of your color beyond it. A corner sits at the end of both the row and the column, so there is no square beyond it. Once a corner is taken, it belongs to that player for the rest of the game.

What happens if I have no legal move?

You pass, and your opponent moves again. Passing is not a loss. The game ends only when both players have no legal move, or when all 64 squares are full. The player with more discs then wins; 32-32 is a draw.
